# feature-film-writer

A complete writing methodology for 90-minute feature films. A short film is a sprint; **a feature film is a marathon**—it needs rises and falls in pacing, it needs quiet scenes that let the audience digest emotion; 90 minutes of sustained high information density will exhaust the audience. This skill is self-contained and does not depend on any other file in this library; if you want to specify "the concrete content criteria for deciding which genre this feature should be—male-oriented / suspense / revenge," you may additionally consult the corresponding genre shell after writing is done, but the genre shell is not a required prerequisite.

## Boundaries: What Not to Do

It does not produce shot lists, shot numbers, shot size / camera movement / composition, transition methods, AI image/video generation prompts, or format constraints of any specific generation engine. It delivers up to a complete, ready-to-read, ready-to-shoot screenplay—and stops there.

## Workflow Discipline

Execute strictly in step order, skipping nothing; after each step is completed, pause and wait for the user's [Approve/Revise/Self-check] instruction; never generate all subsequent steps in one go before handing them to the user; at the end of each step, remind the user that they may request a [Self-check].

Self-check is an internal process—before writing, mentally run through the checklist for the corresponding step; there is no need to show the self-check thinking process in the output, only to ensure the delivered content has passed the checks. The user may still proactively request a [Self-check]; only then do the check items need to be explicitly listed one by one with assessment results.

## Writing Red Lines (Highest Global Priority; No Format May Violate Them)

**Absolutely never**: write psychological description ("he realized," "she understood," "an emotion welled up inside"); write parenthetical hints ("(actually masking nervousness)"); have characters explain settings or themes through dialogue; include preachy passages, forced tearjerkers, sentimental monologues; use excessive metaphors, analogies, or bookish AI-flavored dialogue; write anything a camera cannot capture.

**Must do**: keep dialogue colloquial, like real people talking; replace explanation with action (action is subtext); make dialogue like an iceberg, showing only a tip; approach all content from an audiovisual perspective.

## Core of Audiovisual Writing

**Show, Don't Tell**: If an emotion cannot be conveyed to the audience through a character's physical behavior, that emotion should not appear in the script. Wrong example: "He finally understood his father's painstaking care." Correct approach: He stands there watching for a moment, then takes off his coat and lays it over his father.

**Subtext**: Dialogue is an iceberg; avoid directness and use action, pauses, evasion, and topic shifts to express true meaning. Direct (wrong): "I'm so afraid of losing you." Subtext (right): "Take that coat with you when you go. ... It's cold outside." In a short film the subtext lives in the dialogue; **in a feature film the subtext also lives in the scene sequence**—the juxtaposition of two scenes itself conveys meaning: a wedding immediately followed by a funeral needs no dialogue; the edit itself is the subtext.

**Standard screenplay format**:
```
【Scene X: Location / Time / Brief visual description】

Action/visual description is written directly between lines of dialogue, unmarked; write only what the camera can capture.

Character A: xxxxxxxxx.
Character B: xxxxxxxxx.

Character A does not answer. He turns and walks toward the window, fingers unconsciously tapping the window frame.

Character A: xxxxxxxxx.
```
`【Scene heading】` marks location/time/key visual information in brackets; `Character name:` is followed directly by dialogue; `Character name (OS):` denotes inner monologue/voice-over; action/visual description is unmarked and written directly between lines of dialogue.

## Core Characteristics

- **Length**: 75-120 minutes
- **Characters**: The protagonist has a complete Ghost/Lie/Flaw/Want/Need; supporting characters carry the B-Story
- **Structure**: Choose a structure methodology according to genre (see the structure selection guide below)
- **Arc**: True transformation after multiple false changes
- **Worldbuilding**: Can be complex; revealed in layers
- **Pacing**: Requires a sense of "breathing"—alternating tension and release; it cannot sustain high density throughout

## Key Differences Between Feature Films and Short Films

A short film is a sprint; a feature film is a marathon—it needs rises and falls in pacing, quiet scenes to digest emotion, and comic relief to modulate tension. A short film tells one event; a feature film tells a journey—the protagonist goes through not a single setback but a series of escalating trials, each revealing a deeper truth about the character. In a short film the subtext lives in the dialogue; in a feature film the subtext also lives in the juxtaposition of the scene sequence.

## Core Principle: Film Is a Cross-Section of Real Life

**This is the foundation of the entire methodology.** A story does not run from a character's birth to death; it is a cross-section cut from a complete life: at a particular moment, because of a particular event, this person is forced into an unavoidable situation.

1. **Before the story begins, the world has been running for a long time.** The character has a backstory (Ghost), the world has its era and context, the relationships have accumulated history. Page one of the script is not the character's first day—the character walks into the first scene carrying all of that backstory. This backstory does not need to be fully shown to the audience, but the writer must know it.
2. **The story starts in the middle.** The audience is thrown into a world that is "already in motion"; a good opening "cuts in at the most tension-laden cross-section," and the audience infers the causes backward from the characters' behavior and reactions—more gripping than chronological exposition.
3. **The opening is an attitude.** The first scene defines the whole film's temperament—a commercial film's opening declares its rhythm with high-density plot action; an arthouse film's opening declares its style with a passage of precisely rendered texture of life. Whatever the genre, the opening must be one of the most meticulously designed sequences in the film.
4. **The writer's job is to first build the complete world, then make the cut.** Workflow logic: build first (backstory/worldview/full portrait of the characters), then cut (choose where to begin telling, what to show and what to hide). It is not "what do I want to tell," but "what exists in this world, and through which window do I choose to let the audience look in."

## Dramatic Action: The Smallest Unit of Story

Dramatic action = Goal + Conflict. Not "a person wants to drink water," but "a dehydrated person in the desert searching for water." The goal must carry the urgency of pursuit, and the obstacle must directly oppose the goal. Micro dramatic actions within scenes converge upward into sequence-level core dramatic actions, and the sequence-level ones converge further upward into the film-wide core dramatic action. Test criteria: Is the goal plausible and urgent? Is there a strong conflict between goal and obstacle? Is that conflict enough to drive the story forward?

## Character Depth Design (Feature-Film Level)

Feature-film character design has three more dimensions than short-film design:

**Ghost (backstory wound)**: An event from the character's past that shaped his/her current worldview and behavioral patterns.

**Lie (the lie the character believes)**: A false belief about themselves/the world formed because of the Ghost; this belief drives all of the character's wrong actions, until the story forces him to face it and break it.

**Flaw (character flaw)**: The Lie's concrete manifestation at the behavioral level—stubbornness, avoidance, the need for control, self-sacrificing people-pleasing, and so on.

**Want vs Need**: The Want is the goal that drives the action—something the character is consciously aware of and actively pursuing; the Need is the soul gap the character is unaware of, usually something that can only be obtained after the Lie is broken. The tension between Want and Need is the engine of the character arc—at the story's start the character pursues the Want; by the end, they either obtain the Need (growth arc) or reject the Need (tragic arc/negative arc).

**The Ghost→Lie→Flaw→Want→Need causal chain**: The Ghost (what happened) leads to the Lie (what false thing the character therefore believes), manifests as the Flaw (what this false belief concretely looks like in behavior), drives the Want (what the character therefore actively pursues), and masks the Need (what the character truly needs but cannot see). This chain is more concrete than "inner lack + outer goal"—when writing character bios, you must be able to reason step by step along this chain; if any link is missing, the character will feel thin or their behavior unfounded.

**Character arc**: In short-form content the arc is a single jump (A→B); **a feature film's arc must pass through multiple "false changes"**—the character seems to change, then falls back, until the final true transformation. You must not let the character reach the Need too quickly; that would feel perfunctory.

**Contradiction**: A good character has at least one layer of contradiction—outer presentation vs. inner truth (outward confidence vs. inner insecurity). Contradiction makes a character three-dimensional and gives subtext room to breathe.

## Thematic Argument and B Story
Beyond the core dramatic action, a feature film must also establish the **Thematic Argument**—a proposition that can be debated, with the A Plot and B Plot representing its two opposing sides. When designing the B Story character, this character should carry the opposing side of the thematic argument (if the theme is "trust sustains relationships better than control," the A Plot character might handle crises through control the whole way, while the B Story character should take a path built on trust, even if it looks riskier).

## Backstory and Worldbuilding (Feature Films Must Fully Develop This)

Following the principle that "film is a cross-section of real life," build the complete world before the structural outline.

```
[Era and Setting]
  What era does the story take place in? What is the core conflict/atmosphere of this era?
  What features of this era affect the characters' circumstances?

[Protagonist Backstory (Life Before the Story Begins)]
  Character A: The chain of key events from birth to the story's start—especially Ghost events
  Character B: ...

[Relationship History]
  Character A and Character B: Their relationship history and current state before the story begins
  ...

[World Rules (If Applicable)]
  What are the key differences between this world and the real world?
  Design with the three-layer model: Surface Rules / Operating Logic / Underlying Truth (see below)

[Cross-Section Selection]
  Why cut into the story at this moment?
  What event/change at this moment makes "this moment" the reason the story must begin here?
```

## Layered Worldbuilding Presentation (Three-Layer Model)

Feature films can have multiple layers of complexity, unlike short films, which are "plug-and-play":

```
Surface Rules (shown in the first 30 minutes)—the surface of the worldview that the audience and characters encounter first: "What are the basic ways this world operates"
Operating Logic (shown in Act Two)—the deeper mechanism behind the surface rules: "Why is this world this way," usually revealed through the characters' deeper exploration or the B Story character's perspective
Underlying Truth (revealed in the climax)—the ultimate truth of the entire worldview: "What is this world really," and this revelation should change the audience's understanding of everything that came before
```

**Key Principle**: Each layer's revelation should emerge naturally through the characters' actions and discoveries, not through narration, on-screen text, or characters sitting down to "explain."

## Structural Methodology Selection Guide: Four Methodologies, Chosen by Genre/Style

Choose the structural methodology according to genre and creative intent; mixing is allowed—for example, using the Story Circle as the skeleton and Save the Cat's beats for refinement.

| Genre/Intent | Recommended Methodology | Reason |
|----------|-----------|------|
| Commercial genre films (action, thriller, comedy, horror) | Save the Cat 15 Beats | Beats are precise to the minute, suited to genre films requiring strict pacing control |
| Coming-of-age/Adventure/Metamorphosis | Dan Harmon Story Circle | The eight-step circle naturally fits hero's journey variants of "departure→adventure→return" |
| Dramas with high character complexity and a theme-first approach | McKee's Story Principles | Centered on value shifts and the unity of opposites, suited to deep drama |
| Character-driven art films | Internal Beat Sheet (see Template Four) | Beats are internal state changes rather than external events |
| Hybrid/Undecided | Ask the user's preference first; default to the Story Circle as the skeleton | The Story Circle is versatile enough and easy to understand |

Before proceeding to the structural outline, first confirm with the user which methodology to use—if the user has no clear preference, offer a recommendation based on the table above with a brief rationale, and only expand after the user confirms.

### Template One: Save the Cat 15 Beats

The standard skeleton for commercial genre films, suited to strongly plot-driven material.

**Act One (roughly 25% of runtime)**

1. **Opening Image** (1%): The first shot/scene, showing the protagonist's "old world"—the state before change—in contrast and echo with the final image. This is not just an "establishing shot"—it must be a piece of superb audiovisual content: commercial films use one high-density scene to simultaneously display the character's abilities/personality/the world's rules; art films use a stylized passage to anchor the film's visual temperament. Either way, the Opening Image, taken on its own, should be a piece of content worth watching.
2. **Theme Stated** (around 5%): Some character (usually not the protagonist) inadvertently states the film's theme in dialogue—not a sermon, just an offhand line. At this point the protagonist doesn't grasp its true meaning; they won't truly understand until the Dark Night of the Soul.
3. **Set-Up** (1-10%): Show the protagonist's everyday life, Want, Lie, and what's lacking in their daily life; lay the groundwork for all the characters and relationships that will be used later; the audience needs to care about the protagonist (sympathy or identification).
4. **Catalyst** (around 12%): The event that breaks the balance, an irreversible change—a letter, a phone call, an accident; the protagonist's daily life is shattered.
5. **Debate** (12-25%): The protagonist's hesitation in the face of change—should they accept the challenge? This section must embody the character's Flaw—it is precisely because of the Lie that the protagonist resists change.

**First Half of Act Two (roughly 30% of runtime)**

6. **Break into Two** (around 25%): The protagonist makes the decision and formally steps into the new world/new goal—this is an active choice, not being pushed along.
7. **B Story** (around 30%): Introduce the secondary relationship line that carries the thematic argument (usually a romance/friendship/mentorship line). The A Plot asks "Can the protagonist achieve the goal," while the B Plot asks "Can the protagonist grow."
8. **Fun and Games** (30-55%): The part that delivers on the premise's promise—whatever drew the audience to this genre should be satisfied here, and it is also the section most easily cut into trailers. The protagonist tries to solve new problems with old methods in the new world, with successes and setbacks, at a relatively light overall pace.

**Second Half of Act Two (roughly 30% of runtime)**

9. **Midpoint** (around 50%): The A Plot and B Plot converge here. A false victory (looks like a win but isn't) or a false defeat (looks like a loss but is actually a turning point); the stakes are raised—stakes escalate from the personal level to something larger.
10. **Bad Guys Close In** (55-75%): Internal and external pressures tighten simultaneously. External: the antagonist/obstacles escalate; internal: the team fractures, self-doubt sets in—the protagonist's Flaw begins to exact a real price.
11. **All Is Lost** (around 75%): The lowest point; the protagonist loses what matters most (a person/thing/belief), with a "whiff of death" (literal or metaphorical death); the old methods fail completely.
12. **Dark Night of the Soul** (75-80%): The protagonist confronts their own Lie and Ghost—not awakened by someone else, but seeing the truth for themselves amid the ruins; this is the critical point of the arc's transformation, and no one can resolve it for them.

**Act Three (roughly 15% of runtime)**

13. **Break into Three** (around 80%): The protagonist figures it out—not an upgraded version of the old method, but an entirely new way; the insights of the A Plot and B Plot merge here, and they set out again carrying what was understood in the Dark Night of the Soul.
14. **Finale** (80-99%): The five-step finale—a. gathering the team; b. executing the plan; c. high tower surprise (the plan fails); d. dig deep down (facing the crisis with the newly grown self); e. executing the new plan and winning.
15. **Final Image** (99-100%): A mirror of the Opening Image, showing the protagonist's "new normal"—the state after the change. No need to explain what the character has learned; one image is worth a thousand words.

### Template 2: Dan Harmon Story Circle 8 Steps

Better suited to stories centered on the character's inner journey, with a circular, looping structure.

1. **YOU** (the character is in their comfort zone) → 2. **NEED** (the character wants something) → 3. **GO** (entering an unfamiliar situation) → 4. **SEARCH** (adapting, searching) → 5. **FIND** (getting what they wanted) → 6. **TAKE** (paying the price) → 7. **RETURN** (returning to the familiar situation) → 8. **CHANGE** (the character has changed).

Correspondence with Save the Cat: YOU ≈ Opening Image + Setup, NEED ≈ Theme Stated + the desire side of the Catalyst, GO ≈ Break into Two, SEARCH ≈ Fun and Games, FIND ≈ Midpoint, TAKE ≈ Bad Guys Close In through All Is Lost, RETURN ≈ Break into Three through the Climax, CHANGE ≈ Final Image. The Story Circle emphasizes the "circle" more—the character returns to the starting point but is no longer the same, making it suitable for coming-of-age, root-seeking, and introspective material.

**Unique advantages of the Story Circle**: natural symmetry—the first four steps and the last four steps mirror each other, with step 3 (GO) symmetric to step 7 (RETURN) (departure and return) and step 4 (SEARCH) symmetric to step 6 (TAKE) (searching and paying the price); this symmetry gives the story a sense of "completion"; more flexible than Save the Cat, not bound to precise minute counts; especially suited to character journeys and transformation stories.

### Template 3: McKee's Story Principles

Better suited to serious material emphasizing value shifts and thematic depth.

- **Controlling Idea**: the value proposition of the whole film summed up in a single sentence—"when XX happens, because of XX, XX will/will not happen."
- **Value Shift**: the story must move a core value (life/death, freedom/confinement, love/solitude, etc.) from one polarity to the other, or intensify it in the reverse direction.
- **The Gap**: the rift between the character's expectation and the actual result—the character takes action but gets an unexpected outcome, and this gap forces the character into new, riskier action.
- **Inciting Incident**: the event that upsets the balance of life and arouses the protagonist's desire.
- **Progressive Complications**: a series of escalating conflicts, each one riskier and involving harder choices than the last.
- **Crisis, Choice**: the protagonist faces a genuine dilemma under maximum pressure (not "good vs. bad," but "two goods each with a cost" or "two bads neither of which is perfect").
- **Climax**: the outward-action outcome of the crisis choice.
- **Resolution**: the final state of the value shift.

**Unique value of the McKee method**: it forces every scene to contain a value shift, eliminating dead scenes; the "Gap" concept makes conflict escalate naturally—not by artificially piling on difficulties, but through the naturally deepening disparity between the character's expectations and reality; the Controlling Idea makes the theme concrete and testable—not a vague "about courage," but a debatable judgment in one sentence; especially suited to dramas that require deep character excavation and thematic expression.

Self-check points: Is the Controlling Idea clear and debatable? Does every scene have a value shift? Does the Gap deepen step by step? Is the crisis choice a true dilemma (if the answer is obvious, it is not a true crisis)? Is the climax irreversible?

### Template 4: The Art-Film Internal Beat Sheet

It does not pursue strong plot drive but the gradual evolution of inner states; suited to character-driven, slice-of-life, and auteur-style material.

**The 6-Step Universal Internal Beat Structure**:

1. **Normalcy and the Crack**: the character's everyday life, but some detail hints at unease beneath the surface. The setup of an art film is in no hurry to advance—let the audience linger in the character's world, building atmosphere through sensory details (sound, light, objects).
2. **Disturbance**: a small event breaks the character's self-deception. The catalyst in an art film is usually not a dramatic big event, but a subtle moment that makes it impossible for the character to "keep pretending everything is fine."
3. **Wandering and Avoidance**: the character tries to return to their old life, but the crack has appeared. Lots of "in-between moments"—the character alone, spacing out, doing inconsequential things. **These interludes are not wasted scenes**—what they show is what the character is avoiding facing.
4. **Confrontation**: the character is forced to face what they have long avoided. It might be a quiet conversation, a long-overdue reunion, or simply standing alone somewhere and discovering oneself crying.
5. **Choice or Acceptance**: the climax of an art film is usually not "what was done" but "what was accepted." The character either accepts a painful truth or decides to keep living the lie—both endings work; what matters is the weight of the choice.
6. **Aftershock**: the ending is not an answer but a new question. An art film's Final Image is usually open—the character has changed, but the consequences of the change have not yet arrived.

**5 Style Variants** (chosen by directorial aesthetics; the user may specify one, or one may be recommended based on the story's temperament):

- **Hirokazu Kore-eda style** (daily-life flow; representative works: *Still Walking*, *Shoplifters*, *Nobody Knows*): the setup is extremely slow, and the first 30 minutes are almost entirely everyday life—eating, housework, small talk—with the plot hidden in the details of living; there is no clear "inciting incident"—the crack is not opened by an event but slowly seeps out through the accumulation of the everyday; confrontation is not head-on conflict but emotion suddenly leaking out in a mundane moment—words that stop mid-sentence, suddenly walking a few steps faster, turning to look at a place where someone is no longer there; the ending rests on an utterly ordinary action—clearing the dishes, turning off the light, walking home—the world has not changed, but the audience knows something is different; dialogue is extremely everyday; characters never talk about "big things," yet the shadow of big things is always present; the rhythm is like breathing—even, slow, with no sudden shifts.
- **Wong Kar-wai style** (emotional flow; representative works: *Chungking Express*, *In the Mood for Love*, *Happy Together*): the narrative does not pursue cause and effect; scenes are connected by emotion rather than logic—one scene does not need to "continue" the previous one, only to echo or contrast with it emotionally; heavily repeated visual motifs (motif)—the same space shot over and over at different times, the same action repeated by different characters—repetition creating the texture of time; characters voice their inner selves through narration, but the narration and the image are misaligned—the mouth speaks of one thing while the picture shows another, and the gap between them is the subtext; there is no climax in the traditional sense; the story reaches an emotional saturation point at a certain instant, then closes quietly; time is the protagonist—jumping timelines, expired dates, phones that no longer ring.
- **Yasujiro Ozu style** (still-life flow; representative works: *Tokyo Story*, *An Autumn Afternoon*, *Late Spring*): extremely low camera positions, fixed shots, the camera almost never moving; the frame itself is still, and only the people within it move; "pillow shots"—empty shots (corridors, rooftops, a distant chimney) inserted between scenes; these empty shots do not tell story, they create pauses in which the audience's emotions settle; family relationships are the eternal motif; conflict comes not from external events but from the unbridgeable gap of understanding between generations—parents must in the end accept their children's leaving, and children can never fully understand their parents' sacrifice; the characters are extremely polite and restrained on the surface, but that restraint is itself the greatest subtext; the ending is usually a kind of "acceptance"—neither joy nor sorrow, but a quiet "so it is."
- **Linklater style** (dialogue flow; representative works: the *Before Sunrise* trilogy, *Dazed and Confused*): driven almost entirely by dialogue—dialogue is not a tool, dialogue is the story itself; the rhythm of the dialogue mimics real conversation—it goes off track, circles back, turns suddenly serious and suddenly funny; characters reveal their inner selves naturally in conversation—not through "confession," but through what they choose to talk about, what they avoid, the one topic they linger on a second longer; the constraints of time and space create intimacy instead—two people with limited time must decide what is worth saying; the climax is one right sentence—after all the buildup, when a certain line is spoken, the audience knows "everything is different now"; the ending is usually open—the conversation has ended, but the relationship is still hanging in the air.
- **Bong Joon-ho style**: On the surface it carries the inner rhythm of an arthouse film, but strong genre elements (suspense/thriller/dark humor) are quietly implanted to create sharp emotional turns—the arthouse sensibility and the genre tension alternate without diluting each other, often using sudden violence/absurdity to break the static atmosphere established earlier; the first and second halves can be entirely different genres (in *Parasite*, the first half is comedy and the second half thriller—the genre shift itself is part of the narrative); space as metaphor, where a character's physical position maps directly onto their social position; polysemy of detail, where the same object/action is a comedic prop in the first half and becomes a horror/tragedy prop in the second half; if the user's direction is "a genre film on the outside with social issues at its core," this variant is recommended.

**How to use style variants**: First build the skeleton using the universal inner beat sheet; choose a variant based on the user's reference films or aesthetic preferences; use the variant's "texture" to fill in the concrete writing of each step; variants can be mixed (for example, using Kore-eda's rhythm but Wong Kar-wai's voiceover displacement technique).

**Universal arthouse self-check**: Does every "quiet scene" convey the character's inner state? Is atmosphere built through sensory detail rather than dialogue? Does the character's change come from within rather than being driven by external events? Does the ending leave room for aftershocks? Is there a unified visual/emotional motif running through the entire film? Does it match the texture of the chosen style variant (you cannot write commercial beats under the banner of "arthouse film")? Does the inner displacement genuinely occur, however minute? Did it resist the temptation of "replacing inner gradual change with dramatic events"?

## Subplot Interweaving

Feature films typically run 2-3 storylines simultaneously: **A Plot (main plot)**—the protagonist's external journey, pursuing the Want and confronting obstacles; **B Plot (subplot/B Story)**—usually a relationship that carries the thematic argument, whose progression reflects the changes in the protagonist's inner state; **C Plot (third storyline, optional)**—pacing relief, or a reflection of the theme from another angle.

Interweaving principles: The A and B lines converge at the Midpoint; the B line's progression should mirror the A line—when the A line suffers a setback, the B line offers comfort or a counterexample; do not let the subplot become an independent mini-story—every line must ultimately serve the protagonist's arc.

## Essential Feature-Film Elements (Included by Default Unless the User Specifies Otherwise)

Feature films have enough runtime to accommodate complexity. The following elements are not optional in a feature film—they are standard equipment for good films. If a 90-minute script is missing any of the following, proactively alert the user and suggest adding it.

### Foreshadowing and Payoff

**What foreshadowing is**: A detail (a line of dialogue/a prop/an action/an image) is casually placed early in the story, then made to reappear later with greater meaning.

**Levels of foreshadowing**:
- **Prop foreshadowing**: An object that is mundane in the early section becomes a key prop later. Example: a lighter a character buys on a whim is used in Act Three to ignite an escape signal.
- **Dialogue foreshadowing**: A line that is idle chatter early on acquires an entirely new meaning later. Example: "What I fear most is silence"—in Act One it is characterization; in Act Three, when the character faces silence alone, it becomes an echo.
- **Behavioral foreshadowing**: A habitual action that hints at the character's Ghost. Example: the character always leaves the door open a crack—it is later revealed that she was once locked in a room.
- **Structural foreshadowing**: A seemingly unrelated scene that later proves to hold key information. Example: a passerby's conversation at the start is revealed at the end to be the true mastermind behind everything.

**Foreshadowing rules**: The first appearance must not be too deliberate—the audience should not be aware that "this is foreshadowing"; every piece of foreshadowing must have a payoff—foreshadowing without a payoff is a dangling thread; the payoff should give the audience an "ah, so that's it" feeling, and it should change the audience's understanding of the earlier information; a feature film needs at least 3-5 foreshadowing/payoff pairs, distributed across different levels.

**Foreshadowing register** (filled in during the structural outline stage):
```
Foreshadowing content | Level | First appearance location | Payoff location | Payoff method (how the payoff changes the meaning)
```

### Secondary Characters and Ensemble Design

Secondary characters are not backdrop; in a feature film, every character with lines should have a functional reason to exist.

**Four functions**: **Mirror character**—reflects one side of the protagonist: "if the protagonist had made a different choice, they would have become this person"; **Catalyst character**—an external force that pushes the protagonist to change: mentor/tempter/challenger; **Theme character**—embodies a particular stance within the thematic argument; B Story characters usually serve this function; **Rhythm character**—provides comic relief and emotional variation, giving the audience room to breathe during tense passages.

**Design rules**: Every secondary character should carry at least one function, ideally two; characters without a function should be merged or cut, and two characters serving the same function can be merged into one; secondary characters do not need a complete Ghost/Lie/Flaw, but they need a clear Want and at least one contradictory trait; secondary characters' dialogue styles should be clearly distinguished from the protagonist's, so that the audience can tell who is speaking even with the names covered.

**Additional requirements for ensemble films** (3 or more equally important characters): a relationship matrix among the characters—the relationship status between every pair of characters; POV-switching rules—when to follow whose perspective and whether the switches have rhythm; each character has their own Want/Need, but everyone's pursuit points toward the same theme.

### Hidden Thread Design (Feature-Film Level)

A hidden thread is a storyline that is not in the foreground—the audience may not notice it on a first viewing, but on a second viewing will discover "it was being laid from the very beginning."

**The difference between a hidden thread and a subplot**: A subplot is a second storyline that the audience can see, visibly advancing; a hidden thread runs beneath the surface, operating through foreshadowing, details, and offhand dialogue, until at some moment it surfaces.

**Design method**: First determine the hidden thread's endpoint (what will be revealed when it surfaces) → work backward to determine how many hints must be planted earlier for the reveal to hold up → every hint must carry a surface meaning (the audience reads the surface meaning the first time and the hidden meaning the second) → the hidden thread usually surfaces during the climax or the ending section.

**Quantity recommendation**: 1-2 threads; too many will make the story overly complicated.

## Opening Hook Design

Every film must seize the audience from the opening. The opening hook's task is not "exposition of background" but to **make the audience unable to look away before they even know what the story is**. The opening must contain visual content compelling enough to be captured on camera. An opening hook does not mean an "explosive opening"—quiet can also be a hook: an image so quiet it unsettles, a detail so mundane it puzzles; as long as it makes the audience ask "what's going on?", it is an effective hook.

**Commercial/genre films**: The opening should be an **outstanding scene of high density and high tension** with a dual function—it simultaneously establishes the character's state (ability/personality/circumstances) and the film's genre tone. Classic approaches: use a "routine task" to display the character's ability and style (the jungle adventure that opens *Raiders of the Lost Ark*—the audience immediately knows who this person is, how formidable he is, and what the film's rhythm is); use a "crisis" to throw the character straight into pressure (the bank heist that opens *The Dark Knight*—we witness the villain's rules before we ever meet the protagonist); use a "result-first" image to create suspense (we see the bizarre outcome first, and the whole film answers "how did this happen?").

**Arthouse/character-driven films**: The opening should be a **passage that represents the style and temperament of the whole film**—it does not need tension, but it needs distinctiveness. Classic approaches: use a highly stylized long take to establish atmosphere (the backstage long take that opens *Birdman*); use a stretch of seemingly unrelated everyday detail to hint at the crack in the character's interior (Kore-eda style—the opening is just cooking and eating, but one detail feels "off"); anchor the film's visual system with the first appearance of a visual motif (Wong Kar-wai style—an image/action from the opening will recur throughout the film).

**Opening hook self-check**: Does the opening's first image have visual impact or a sense of suspense? Will the audience form a concrete question within the first 30 seconds? Does the opening create tension while establishing the state (rather than "first exposition, then start")? Is the opening's genre tone consistent with the whole film? If the opening were lifted out on its own, would it stand as a compelling piece of audiovisual content?

## Dual-Track Rhythm System
Rhythm runs on two parallel tracks that can be in sync or offset from each other, and offset itself is a powerful narrative tool. **External plot rhythm** (event density and intensity: tight = high-density events/fast cutting/multi-person dialogue/chases/confrontations/revelations, loose = everyday scenes/solitary moments/transitions/scenery/silence); **internal emotional rhythm** (character emotional fluctuations: heavy = confessions/betrayals/loss/fear/ecstasy/despair, light = banter/everyday small talk/small humor/quiet companionship). Rule of thumb: after every stretch of high-intensity plot, you need a stretch of "breathing" for the audience to digest.

Four offset combinations: loose plot + heavy emotion (two people eating a quiet meal, nothing happens, but the audience knows one of them is leaving tomorrow—the calmer the visuals, the heavier the emotion); tight plot + light emotion (an intense car chase set to a light pop song); tight plot + heavy emotion (the climax sequence—dense events and explosive emotion, the peak of the whole film, must not last too long); loose plot + light emotion (pure breathing scenes, usually placed before or after the climax).

At the structural outline and scene breakdown stage, you should draw a "rhythm wave chart," marking each segment's external plot intensity and internal emotional intensity, and checking the contrast between rises and falls. Avoid: both tracks sitting low at the same time for long stretches; both tracks sitting high at the same time for long stretches; either track rising monotonically with no fall back.

## Duration Estimation and Rhythm Control

**Estimation method**: 1 page of standard screenplay format ≈ 1 minute of finished film; for a Chinese script, roughly 200-250 characters of dialogue plus action description ≈ 1 minute. Estimate pure dialogue scenes by the character count of the dialogue; estimate action/montage scenes by the number of shots (each distinct shot roughly 3-5 seconds); silence/emotion scenes are easy to underestimate—a character just standing still may need 10-15 seconds in the finished film to carry enough emotional weight; transitions take 2-5 seconds each.

**Scene duration annotation format**:
```
【Scene 3: Hospital Corridor / Night / Fluorescent lights buzzing】 (approx. 1 min 30 sec)
  Plot rhythm: medium (one key conversation)
  Emotional rhythm: heavy (the character faces the truth for the first time)
```

**Full-film duration budget (using a 100-minute film as an example)**: setup 25 minutes / first half of development 30 minutes / second half of development 25 minutes / climax 15 minutes / resolution 5 minutes. These are reference proportions and can be flexibly adjusted, but be aware of what you're doing.

**Rhythm diagnosis**: Is the climax sequence long enough? Is the setup too long? Are there stretches of more than 3 consecutive minutes in a single emotional register? Is there enough breathing room between two high-emotion scenes? Does the film's longest single scene have sufficient justification to occupy that much runtime?

## Cross-Format Technique Borrowing (What Feature Films Can Borrow)

**Borrow from concept films**: a high-concept twist can be scaled down to a single scene in a feature—when you need a "punch," ask whether the scene's last second can make the audience re-understand its first second. The concept purity test can be applied to a feature's Logline—if it can't be stated clearly in one sentence, the core concept isn't sharp enough. The progressive-collapse structure can be used for "villain closing in" sequences—pressure is ratcheted up level by level until the breaking point. From the audiovisual-language arsenal, split screens/jump-cut montage/image-sound mismatch/silence/color restriction are more effective than conventional narration when borrowed for specific feature segments (time-compression passages, time-skip passages, ironic scenes, post-climax), but use them only in specific segments—don't use the same flashy technique across the whole film.

**Borrow from short films**: information-density montage for the Fun and Games section of the first half of Act Two (the protagonist trial-and-erroring in the new world); extreme single-variable extrapolation for the climax sequence (strip away all distractions and focus on the core choice); zero-degree expression (the bigger the disaster, the flatter the tone) for the darkest moment.

**Borrow from episodic series**: end-of-episode hook logic for feature sequence transitions—leaving a small hook at the end of each sequence has more pull than an "and then..." transition; arc-budget thinking for planning false change in a feature—a character shouldn't reach real change by minute 30; plan several false changes and regressions; information release schedules for managing complex worldviews; continuity-tracking thinking for writing the second half of a feature—before writing Act Three, first make a table of each character's current physical state/emotional state/known information.

## Memory Checkpoint System

A feature screenplay runs roughly 15,000-25,000 characters; during the writing process, scenes written earlier, foreshadowing planted, and shifts in characters' states may get "forgotten" by the time you write later sections, creating contradictions between front and back. Memory checkpoints solve this by periodically generating structured "snapshots."

**Trigger timing**: trigger once after completing each Sequence (2-3 scenes), roughly one checkpoint per 10-15 minutes of finished-film content. In addition, proactively trigger when the user starts a new conversation but wants to continue the previous work, when so much has been written that earlier details are starting to blur, or when the user explicitly asks to "summarize the progress so far."

**Checkpoint template (feature-level, adding Subplot/arc/worldview/structural position on top of the generic template)**:
```
📌 Memory Checkpoint | Feature | [Current Position]

【Completed Progress】which step we are on / which Sequence or scene has been written
【Story Core (unchanging)】Logline / thematic argument / core dramatic action: goal→obstacle
【Characters' Current State】[Character name]: physical state/emotional state/what they currently know/what they currently want
【Summary of Completed Segments】Scene 1: one-sentence summary + core value shift; ...
【Active Threads】threads awaiting follow-up and their status
【Foreshadowing Status】planted, not yet paid off: [content]→planned payoff at [location]; paid off: [content]✅
【Rhythm Status】previous segment's plot/emotional rhythm → suggested direction for the next segment

【Subplot Status】A Plot progress / B Plot progress / C Plot progress (if any)
【Arc Progress】which stage of the arc the character is currently in; whether the Lie has been questioned and how many times; where the last false change occurred
【Worldview Layers Revealed】which layer the audience knows so far; where the next layer is planned to be revealed
【Structural Position】which beat we are currently in (which stage of STC/Story Circle/McKee); how much runtime remains until the next key beat

【Next to Write】what the next segment to write is, and what its core dramatic action is
```

**Compression principles**: record conclusions, not processes; record changes, not constants; record only information needed later; active threads and foreshadowing are the most important and must be listed in full at every checkpoint.

**How to use**: when writing, review the most recent checkpoint before starting each new segment; across conversations, ask the user for the previous checkpoint's content to restore context; during script doctoring, read all the checkpoints straight through in order to quickly spot foreshadowing that was planted but never paid off, a character state that suddenly jumps (a continuity error), rhythm that hasn't changed for a long time, or a Subplot that hasn't advanced for a long time.

## The Eight-Step Workflow for Features

Features use the same eight-step workflow skeleton as shorts, but each step goes deeper and demands more.

**Step 1 (Cracking the Premise) additional requirements**: beyond the core dramatic action, you must also establish the thematic argument—a debatable proposition, with the A Plot and B Plot respectively representing its two opposing sides.

**Step 2 (Synopsis Draft) additional requirements**: the feature synopsis should be a **one-page Treatment** (300-500 words), not just a paragraph. It should include: the protagonist's core dilemma, the catalytic event, the direction of the midpoint reversal, the rough shape of the climax, and the emotional tone of the ending. If there is a B Story, the Treatment should make the existence of both lines felt. The Treatment's voice must convey the genre temperament of the whole film—after reading it, you should know "what kind of movie this is."

**Step 3 (Characters) additional requirements**: add the three dimensions of Ghost/Lie/Flaw; design the B Story character (who carries the opposing side of the thematic argument); design all secondary characters and label each one's function (mirror/catalyst/theme/rhythm); build the relationship web among the multiple characters; for an ensemble film, design a character relationship matrix.

**Step 4 (Backstory and Worldview)—must be fully developed for features**: follow the principle that "film is a cross-section of real life," and build the complete world before the structural outline (see the template above).
**Step 5 (Structural Outline) Additional Requirements**: Confirm with the user which methodology to use, based on the structure selection guide; if the user has no explicit preference, recommend one based on the subject matter and explain the reasoning; opening hook design—the first paragraph of the outline must be a piece of sufficiently compelling audiovisual content, which needs to be marked separately and quality-confirmed; mark the interweaving points of the Subplots; mark the reveal timing for each layer of the worldview; time budget—mark an estimated duration for each structural segment; dual-track pacing annotation—for each segment, mark the external plot intensity and the internal emotional intensity; foreshadowing planning—fill in the foreshadowing registry, planning at least 3-5 foreshadowing/payoff pairs; hidden thread planning—if there is a hidden thread, mark its hint points in each segment.

**Step 6 (Scene Breakdown) Additional Requirements**: Introduce the Sequence concept—2-3 scenes form one sequence, and each sequence has its own mini "setup→conflict→resolution"; a 90-minute feature has roughly 8-12 sequences; mark an estimated duration for each scene; mark the dual-track pacing state for each scene (plot pacing loose/medium/tight + emotional pacing light/medium/heavy); after summing up the scene durations, check whether they match the segment time budget from Step 5; **after Step 6 is complete, trigger a memory checkpoint**, locking in global information before entering the writing phase.

**Step 7 (Scene Writing) Additional Requirements**: When writing, pay attention to the natural placement of foreshadowing—it must not be too deliberate; secondary characters' dialogue styles must be distinct from the protagonist's; backstory information should be naturally scattered through dialogue and visual cues, not delivered in a concentrated exposition; **after completing each Sequence (2-3 scenes), trigger a memory checkpoint**—review character states, active threads, foreshadowing status, and pacing direction.

**Step 8 (Script Doctor) Additional Requirements**: Check whether the A/B/C Plots are effectively interwoven; check whether worldview reveals are natural; check whether the arc's "false change" is sufficient—the character should not reach the Need too quickly; opening hook quality check—pull out the opening separately: is it one of the most carefully crafted segments of the entire film; pacing diagnosis—use scene duration totals to check the pacing of the entire film; foreshadowing audit—check the foreshadowing registry item by item to confirm every piece of foreshadowing has a payoff; secondary character audit—confirm every character with lines has a function.

## Common Pitfalls in Feature Films

- ❌ Saggy second act ("saggy second act") → Ensure the Midpoint is strong enough to split the second act in two
- ❌ Character arc too linear (going straight from Lie to Truth) → Requires at least one regression after a "false change"
- ❌ B Story disconnected (unrelated to the A Plot) → The B Story must map the theme
- ❌ Climax is just "a bigger fight" → The climax must be the externalization of the character's inner choice
- ❌ Worldview dumped all at once → Release it in layers, letting the audience linger long enough on each layer
- ❌ Foreshadowing too deliberate (the audience can tell at a glance "this thing will be used later") → The first appearance of a piece of foreshadowing must have a plausible surface reason
- ❌ Planted foreshadowing but forgot the payoff → Audit item by item using the foreshadowing registry
- ❌ Secondary characters are mere tools (appearing only to give the protagonist one piece of information and then vanishing) → Every secondary character must have at least one Want of their own
- ❌ Monotonous pacing throughout (constant tension or constant slowness) → Use dual-track pacing annotations to check for rises and falls
- ❌ No duration estimation, causing the climax segment to get compressed → Start the time budget as early as Step 3
- ❌ No breathing room between two high-emotion scenes → Use the dual-track pacing chart to check the tension and release of emotional pacing
- ❌ Plot pacing and emotional pacing always in sync → Deliberately creating some misalignment (loose plot + heavy emotion, or tight plot + light emotion) is more sophisticated
