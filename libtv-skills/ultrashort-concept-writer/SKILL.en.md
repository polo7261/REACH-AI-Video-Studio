# ultrashort-concept-writer

A complete writing methodology for 1-3 minute concept ultrashorts. **This is pure "idea delivery"**—it doesn't rely on story; it hits the audience through the sharpness of the concept itself and the impact of its audiovisual language. This skill is self-contained and does not depend on any other file in this repository; if you need to specify the concrete content-judgment criteria for which genre this concept ultrashort should cover—male-oriented fiction / suspense / revenge—you may additionally consult the corresponding genre shell after finishing the concept, but the genre shell is not a required prerequisite.

## Boundaries: What Not to Do

Does not produce storyboard sheets, shot numbers, shot sizes/camera movements/compositions, transition methods, AI image/video generation prompts, or format constraints for any specific generation engine. Output goes up to a complete written script that can be read directly and shot directly.

## Workflow Discipline

Execute strictly in step order, no skipping steps; after each step is completed, pause and wait for the user's [approve/revise/self-check] instruction; never generate all subsequent steps at once and then hand them over; after each step, remind the user that they may request a [self-check].

Self-checking is an internal process—before writing, mentally run through the checklist for the corresponding step; there is no need to display the self-check reasoning in the output, only to ensure the delivered content has already passed the checks. The user may still proactively request a [self-check], and only then should the check items be explicitly listed one by one with assessment results.

## Writing Red Lines (Global Highest Priority, No Format May Violate Them)

**Absolutely do not**:
- Write psychological description ("he realized," "she understood," "a surge within")—especially fatal in ultrashorts; every second of the 3 minutes should be imagery, not explanation
- Write parenthetical hints ("(actually masking nervousness)")
- Have characters explain the setting or concept rules through dialogue
- Preachy passages, forced tear-jerking, sentimental monologues
- Overly metaphorical sentences, analogies, bookish AI-flavored dialogue
- Write anything a camera cannot capture

**Must do**:
- Dialogue should be colloquial, like real people talking (ultrashorts often have very little or no dialogue, so cherish every line even more)
- Use action instead of explanation (action is subtext)
- All content should proceed from an audiovisual perspective

## The Core of Audiovisual Writing

**Show, Don't Tell**: If an emotion/rule cannot be conveyed to the audience through visuals or sound, it should not appear in the script. Wrong example: "He finally understood the rules of this world." Correct approach: He reaches out to touch that light; the light suddenly vanishes, and every lamp in the room goes dark at the same time.

**Subtext**: Dialogue is an iceberg; the spoken words are only the surface. Ultrashorts inherently have extremely low dialogue density—once you do write a line, make it carry double meaning; it must not be a plain rule explanation.

**Dialogue style** (if there is dialogue): Colloquial, with different characters speaking in different ways; avoid excessive metaphors and bookish AI flavor; don't let characters say things both parties already know—ultrashort dialogue is already extremely sparse, so every line that appears must withstand this test.

**Standard script format**:
```
[Scene X: Location / Time / Brief visual description]

Action/visual description written directly between lines of dialogue, without markers, only what the camera can capture.

Character A: xxxxxxxxx.
```
`Character name (OS):` indicates inner monologue/voiceover, used only when the audiovisual technique itself requires it (e.g., essay-style voiceover-type How-to-Tell).

## Step 1: Determine the Path—What-If or How-to-Tell

The two paths have completely different methodologies; the fork happens at step one—before entering creation, first confirm which one the user wants:

- **What-If type**: Assume a counterintuitive premise and show its consequences and extrapolations. The core weapon is **the purity and combination of the concept**.
- **How-to-Tell type**: Retell a known thing in an unexpected narrative way—the content itself is not the selling point; **the narrative form itself** is.

## Core Characteristics (Shared by Both Types)

- **Duration**: 1-3 minutes
- **Characters**: Functional archetypes, no depth or backstory needed. Characters are carriers of the concept; there can be no characters at all.
- **Arc**: Usually no character arc. If there is one, it is only an instant cognitive flip, not a complete A-to-B transformation.
- **Worldview**: Plug-and-play; the audience must understand the rule within 5 seconds—no explaining, show the consequences directly through visuals.
- **Pacing**: Transmitting information from the very first frame; zero wasted shots.
- **Audiovisual language**: Ultrashorts are a legitimate space for showing off technique—the audiovisual form itself can become part of the content; in features/shorts this must be used with restraint, but in ultrashorts it can be used freely.

# Part A: What-If Type

## The Essence of What-If

A good What-If is not unbridled fantasy, but **a magnifying glass held up to reality**—using a fictional rule to let the audience see what they turn a blind eye to in reality: some real but overlooked social mechanism, human weakness, or absurd logic, magnified to the point of being impossible to ignore. To judge whether a What-If qualifies: strip away the fictional rule shell—is the real anxiety it points to still valid, still stinging?

## Concept Combination: The Core Ideation Method

The sharpest What-Ifs are often not a single concept, but the chemical reaction produced by the collision of two unrelated concepts.

**Combination method one: concept grafting**—take the operating mechanism of concept A + the application scenario of concept B. Example: "kill line" (game elimination mechanic) + AI anxiety → "The company uses AI to score every employee; anyone scoring below the kill line finds nothing but a USB drive left at their workstation the next day"; game respawn mechanic + workplace layoffs → "Laid-off people can insert a coin to respawn, but each respawn halves their salary." Steps: list A's core mechanism → list what people care about most in B's domain → stuff A's rules into B's scenario → test whether this combination reveals a real problem in B's scenario.

**Combination method two: temporal-spatial displacement**—place a character/technology/institution into an era or place it does not belong to. Example: Iron Man + the 1990s layoff wave → "A senior engineer at a Northeast military factory is laid off and builds armor from scrap metal in his garage, then discovers its power consumption exceeds the electricity bill of the entire street"; ChatGPT + the imperial examination → "A candidate brings a bronze box that can compose couplets into the examination hall; the chief examiner discovers the eight-legged essays written by the box are better than those of any candidate." The power of this approach: temporal-spatial displacement lets the audience see two eras at once—understanding the absurdity of the present requires a mirror from the past, and vice versa.

**Combination method three: metaphor materialization**—turn an abstract social anxiety into a physical rule the camera can capture. Method: take abstract anxiety A → find its "minimal action" at the behavioral level → turn that action into a mandatory physical rule. Example: the minimal action of "involution" is "if others work overtime, I must work overtime" → physical rule: "Everyone's lights must be brighter than their neighbors'; if not bright enough, the power is cut"; the minimal action of "appearance anxiety" is "comparing in the mirror" → "The mirror scores your face, and the score displays on your forehead where others can see it."

**Combination method four: ironic juxtaposition**—find two things that are superficially similar but essentially contradictory, and let them coexist in the same scene. Example: "pets" and "livestock farming" → "A person lovingly feeds his cat; right next door to the cat food factory is a slaughterhouse"; "anti-consumerism" and "consumption" → "A person wears a T-shirt printed with 'Reject consumerism'; the T-shirt is priced at 399."

**Combination method five: rule inversion**—find a rule people take for granted, flip it, and extrapolate the consequences. Example: "the older you get, the more mature you are" inverted to "people are born old, grow younger as they live, and die as infants"; "memory fades over time" inverted to "memory doesn't fade, but you can delete it deliberately—the price is that every deleted memory permanently takes away the corresponding emotion."

**Testing concept combinations**: Does the combination produce a "1+1>2" effect (if combining the two concepts isn't more interesting than either one alone, abandon it)? Can the audience understand the rule within 5 seconds? Does it point to a real social sentiment or human weakness? Is there room for extrapolation (what happens when the rule is pushed to its limit)? Does it have twist potential (can it turn around at the end and slap the audience in the face)?

## The Five Structures of What-If
**Structure A: Classic Three-Part Format (Concept Presentation → Extrapolation → Twist)**—the most versatile. 1. Concept presentation (first 15-30 seconds; a single image/action lets the audience instantly understand the rule—don't explain, show the consequences directly; ideally a visual hook that makes people go "Huh?"). 2. Extrapolation (middle 30-90 seconds; show 2-5 consequence slices, each 5-15 seconds; the slices must escalate—each one more extreme/more absurd/or more mundane than the last). 3. Twist (final 15-30 seconds; one image or one line completely overturns the understanding established earlier; the twist must be an extension of the concept's own logic, not a forced turn).

**Structure B: Progressive Collapse**—suited to "rules that keep escalating until they break down." Rule establishment (~20 seconds; the rule in its basic form looks fairly reasonable) → First escalation (~20 seconds; stricter, but people have adapted) → Second escalation (~20 seconds; escalated to the absurd, but people are still adapting) → Collapse point (~20 seconds; the rule is pushed to its logical limit and the system collapses instantly; or everyone has fully adapted to the most absurd state, and what collapses isn't the rule but the audience's understanding) → Afterimage (final 10 seconds; a quiet image after the collapse—everyday life amid the ruins).

**Structure C: Dual-Thread Juxtaposition**—suited to reversal-juxtaposition concepts. Show two threads that both look normal/warm/beautiful at the same time, advancing in visual parallel (split screen / cross-cutting / foreground and background within the same shot). At some moment the audience suddenly realizes the two threads are actually two sides of the same thing, or that Thread A's beauty is built on Thread B's cruelty. That moment of "realizing" is the twist—no character needs to say it out loud.

**Structure D: Cycle/Loop Format**—suited to "an inescapable predicament" or "history repeating." Starting point (State A) → development → apparent resolution → back to State A. Key: the second time the audience sees State A, their understanding has changed—the first time it felt like a normal beginning; the second time they realize it's a cage they can never escape. You can cut to black at the start of the second loop, letting the audience realize on their own: "Here we go again."

**Structure E: Reverse-Chronology Reveal**—suited to "the truth was in front of the audience all along, but they didn't see it." First show the result (a strange, incomprehensible image) → trace backward to the cause, revealing step by step how the image came to be; each reveal makes the image more comprehensible but also more horrifying/absurd/ironic → the final reveal lets the audience understand everything; looking back at the first image, its meaning is completely different.

## The What-If Workflow

**Step 1: Concept Forging**. Determine whether the user's input is a single concept or needs combination; if the given direction isn't sharp enough, use the five combination methods to generate 3-5 candidates. Output: one-sentence concept / combination method / the real anxiety or human nature the concept points to / extrapolation potential (what is the most absurd image when the rule is pushed to its limit) / twist potential (what is the biggest possible cognitive reversal) / suggested structure A-E / suggested audiovisual language. Check: Can the rule be stated in one sentence? Can it be shown on screen within 5 seconds? Does it point to a real social sentiment or human weakness? Is there room to extrapolate at least 3 escalating consequence slices? Does it have twist potential? Can it be told within 3 minutes (if too complex, downgrade to the short-film format)?

**Step 2: Structure and Audiovisual Design**. Choose the structure type, pick 1-2 core techniques from the audiovisual-language arsenal (see Part C), and expand into a structural outline with time annotations, marking each section with its audiovisual technique and dual-track rhythm (loose/medium/tight for plot, light/medium/heavy for emotion). Check: Does the concept presentation let the audience get the rule within 5 seconds? Do the extrapolation slices escalate layer by layer rather than sit side by side? Does the twist hold up logically? Does the twist make the audience want to go back and rewatch? Do the audiovisual techniques reinforce the content? Is the total runtime within 1-3 minutes?

**Step 3: Full Script**. Radically minimal; every single word must exist within the frame; zero explanation, purely visual storytelling (unless the audiovisual technique itself calls for narration); very little dialogue, possibly none at all; the twist lands on the final image or the final line; audiovisual descriptions must be concrete (not "use a long take," but "the camera starts on the character's hands, slowly pulls back, and reveals the whole room—every workstation is empty"). After writing, provide: estimated runtime per section, a self-assessment of whether the twist is strong enough, 2-3 alternative twist options, and alternative audiovisual techniques.

# Part B: The How-to-Tell Type

## The Essence of How-to-Tell

Don't change "what to tell"—change "how to tell it." Premise: the audience already knows, or can quickly grasp, the story's content; the content itself is not the selling point—**the narrative method itself** is. The power lies in this: when a known thing is retold in an entirely new way, the audience is forced to re-understand it from an angle they've never had before. **Form is content.**

## Five Creative Methods

**Method 1: Perspective Swap**—tell the story from an impossible point of view. Examples: telling a war from the bullet's perspective; telling a day in the life of someone who lives alone from the takeout bag's perspective; telling the history of human civilization from Earth's perspective (Earth as a host infected by parasites); telling the life of a meme sticker from its own perspective—being sent/received/screenshotted/forwarded.

**Method 2: Form Simulation**—repackage the story using an existing form (file format/interface/game/manual, etc.). Examples: using the "product manual" format to tell a marriage (Product name: Marriage; Shelf life: uncertain; Side effects: see appendix); using the "game walkthrough" format to tell a day in the life of an office worker; using "surveillance camera footage" to tell a love story that happens in a convenience store; using "search history" to tell one person's psychological journey from diagnosis to acceptance.

**Method 3: Time Surgery**—accelerate, decelerate, reverse, loop, jump, or juxtapose time. Examples: compress one person's entire life into 2 minutes, a few seconds per life stage; stretch 1 second of action into 3 minutes (what happens in the 1 second a bullet leaves the barrel, a glass shatters, a tear slides down); the same street corner across 100 years, cut once every 10 years, camera position fixed; one person's morning played forward + the same person's night played in reverse, the two timelines converging at noon.

**Method 4: Scale Jumping**—jump between the very large and the very small, using scale contrast for impact. Examples: start with one cell dividing, pull progressively out to a person, a city, a planet, a galaxy, then cut back to the cell; a family argument → cut to Earth seen from outer space → cut back to the argument, which suddenly looks ridiculous; the journey of a single grain of rice—from rice paddy to dinner table to trash can to landfill to groundwater and back to the rice paddy.

**Method 5: Rule Constraint**—impose a constraint on the storytelling itself; it is precisely this constraint that generates the creativity. Examples: the entire film uses only one fixed camera setup, with no editing; not a single person appears in the entire film, only spaces and objects; the entire film is a black screen with only sound (or the reverse); the entire film is made up of phone screenshots / chat logs / notification bars.

## The Structure of How-to-Tell

Don't use the three-part "concept presentation → extrapolation → twist" structure; the structure is determined by **the narrative form itself**—the form is the structure. General rhythm: 1. Form establishment (first 20-30 seconds; let the audience understand how this film is going to tell its story; the audience's first reaction should be "Oh? Told this way?"). 2. Form deepening (middle 60-120 seconds; unfold the content within this formal framework, and as the content advances the form itself progresses—escalating, mutating, or exposing new meanings, not repeating the same technique). 3. Convergence of form and content (final 15-30 seconds; the audience realizes that form and content are one—"telling it this way" is itself part of the story; the best ending is when the form itself completes the twist).

**Example**: using "search history" to tell one person's journey from diagnosis to acceptance—form establishment: the screen scrolls through search entries, "causes of headaches," "how to relieve migraines"; form deepening: the searches grow heavier, "how much does a brain CT cost," "glioma survival rate"; convergence: the final search is "good places to eat nearby"—form (search history) and content (accepting reality) merge in this one search.

## The How-to-Tell Workflow
**Step 1: Form Discovery**. First confirm the content/theme the user wants to tell, then use five creative methods to generate 3-5 form options. Output: content/theme / narrative form / creative method (perspective swap / form imitation / time surgery / scale jump / rule constraint) / the relationship between form and content (why telling it this way is better, and whether it would work without it) / the form's potential for progression / recommended audiovisual language. Checks: Does the form fit the content deeply (not just "looks cool," but "this is the only way it can be told")? Does the form have room for progression (you can't ride one technique all the way through)? Does the form itself have twist potential? Can the audience grasp the narrative form within 15 seconds? If the form were removed and the content told in a conventional way, would it lose its impact (if not, change the form)?

**Steps 2 and 3**: Same as the What-If structure design and full-script process, with these additional self-checks: Do form and content converge at the ending (not "the form packages the content," but "the form itself becomes part of the content")? Does the form itself change/escalate/mutate from start to finish?

# Part C: Audiovisual Language Arsenal (Common to Both Types)

Ultra-short films are a legitimate space for showing off audiovisual language — within 3 minutes, audiences have extremely high tolerance for formal experimentation. The techniques below can be used alone or in combination, but a film should focus on 1-2 core techniques pushed to the extreme; don't cram in five tricks and turn it into a technique showcase.

**Camera language**: One-take (no cuts from start to finish; the effect is realism/immersion/the irreversibility of time; suited to concepts that need a "sense of presence"); fixed-camera time passage (the camera doesn't move while time accelerates within the frame; the effect is a contrast between change and changelessness; suited to concepts spanning long periods of time); continuous tracking shot (the camera moves through space at a constant speed; the effect is the revelation of space; suited to concepts where the space itself carries information, e.g., tracking from a mansion to a slum); first-person POV (the effect is extreme immersion; suited to perspective-swap How-to-Tell); macro/microscopic shots (shooting at scales invisible to the naked eye; the effect is making the familiar strange; suited to scale-jump How-to-Tell).

**Editing/time**: Jump-cut montage (rapid time jumps within the same frame; the effect is time compression and the absurdity of repetition; suited to showing "day after day"); split screen (the frame divided into 2-4 zones shown simultaneously; the effect is contrast/juxtaposition/parallel timelines; suited to dual-track juxtaposition structures); cross-cutting (rapid switching between two storylines; the effect is accelerating contrast and building tension; suited to two seemingly unrelated things ultimately revealed to be connected); reverse playback (the effect is making familiar actions strange, with results appearing before causes; suited to time-surgery or loop structures); freeze frame/time freeze (the effect is forcing the audience to notice a detail; suited to emphasizing the twist moment amid a dense flow of visual information).

**Composition**: Symmetrical framing (the effect is a sense of order/ritual/unease; suited to depicting concepts of institutionalization); foreground/background narration within the frame (one thing happening in the foreground while another happens in the background, each annotating the other; the effect is juxtaposition without editing); color restriction (the whole film uses only a limited palette; the effect is that color itself becomes a narrative element; suited to when color is directly tied to the concept, e.g., red = danger/love, gray = systems/oppression).

**Sound**: Audio-visual dislocation (the image shows A while the sound belongs to B; the effect is a collision of two layers of information; suited to satirical contrast, e.g., peaceful everyday images with the sound of disaster figures from a news broadcast); amplified ambient sound (blasting everyday ambient noise to an abnormal volume; the effect is the familiar becoming unsettling); silence (no sound at all at the key moment; the effect is forcing the audience to concentrate; suited to sudden silence after a high-density flow of information — the silence itself is the climax).

**Interface/medium**: Screen recording (the entire film is a phone/computer screen recording; the effect is an extreme sense of the contemporary; suited to form-imitation How-to-Tell); fake documents/mockumentary (pretending to be a real documentary/news report/tutorial video; the effect is wrapping an absurd core in a shell of authenticity; suited to a What-If that needs a "serious" shell for contrast); data visualization (telling the story through charts/data/statistical visual language; the effect is that the cooler the numbers, the more shocking the content).

**How to choose**: Form serves content — settle the concept first, then pick the technique; one film focuses on 1-2 core techniques; the technique needs progression (escalation/mutation/broken at the end, e.g., a fixed camera throughout the whole film whose final shot suddenly moves); the technique itself can become an instrument of the twist (the audiovisual form itself also twists, e.g., normal speed throughout the whole film, then a sudden acceleration in the last second until the whole world flies past before your eyes).

## Output Format (What Should Be Delivered After Writing)

1. **One-sentence concept**: (the rule of the What-If, or the form of the How-to-Tell)
2. **Concept/form checklist**: (confirm item by item against the checks for the corresponding type above)
3. **Structure outline**: (segment by segment, annotated with duration/audiovisual technique/dual-track pacing)
4. **Full script**: (standard format: scene headings + action description + dialogue/or narration)
5. **Ending suggestions**: a self-assessment of whether the twist lands + 2-3 alternative options

## Common Pitfalls

- ❌ The concept requires lengthy explanation to be understood → swap the concept or downgrade to a short film
- ❌ The extrapolation section merely repeats the concept rather than progressing → each slice must go further than the last
- ❌ The twist is a hard turn with no logical connection to what came before → the twist must be an extension of the concept's own logic
- ❌ Trying to cram a complete character arc into 3 minutes → abandon the arc, focus on the concept
- ❌ Dialogue that "explains the concept" → cut it, show it visually
- ❌ Audiovisual technique unrelated to the concept ("I wanted to use split screen, so I used split screen") → form must reinforce content
- ❌ Too many audiovisual techniques in one film → focus on 1-2 and push them to the extreme
- ❌ After combining concepts, the two remain independent with no chemical reaction → the combination must be 1+1>2
- ❌ The How-to-Tell form is mere "packaging," and the story works fine without it → the form must be irreplaceable
- ❌ Great concept but jumping straight to the twist without extrapolation → the extrapolation section is the process of the audience "getting into" the story; it cannot be skipped

## Relationship to the Genre-Shell Skill

This skill only governs "how to write in the 1-3 minute format"; it does not cover "the specific criteria for deciding which genre — male-oriented/suspense/revenge — this story should take." If you need genre-specific criteria (what counts as satisfying, what counts as scary), then after finishing the concept outline you can additionally consult the "this episode/segment is a dud" checklist in the corresponding genre-shell skill in this library; even without consulting it, you can fully complete an ultra-short film on your own. The two are orthogonal axes, not a dependency.
